}

fn config_for_stl(stl_path: &str) -> config::Config {
    config::parse_config_from(["brepper", stl_path, "--stage=1", "-q"]).unwrap()
}

fn config_for_compare(stl_path: &str, step_path: &str) -> config::Config {
    let mut config =
        config::parse_config_from(["brepper", stl_path, "--compare", step_path, "--stage=1", "-q"])
            .unwrap();
    config.load_compare_step().unwrap();
    config
}

Your intuition is right *in general*, but the rounded cube is a special case where it breaks down. The key geometric fact:

**On a rounded cube with fillet radius r, the cylindrical fillet edges and the spherical corners have the same radius r, and the sphere center at each corner is equidistant (distance = r) from every vertex on the adjacent cylinder fillets.**

Consider one corner sphere centered at point C with radius 2. The three cylinder fillets meeting at that corner each have radius 2, and each cylinder's axis is offset exactly 2mm from the corner. A cylinder fillet face's vertices lie on a cylinder of radius 2. But those same vertices also lie on a sphere of radius 2 centered at C — because the cylindrical surface and spherical surface are *tangent* along their shared boundary, and near the boundary the cylinder surface curves toward the sphere center at exactly the right rate.

More precisely: a cylinder fillet on a rounded cube is a 90° arc of a circle with radius r. The sphere at the corner is also radius r. The fillet meets the sphere tangentially. The cylinder axis is at distance r from two adjacent flat faces, and the sphere center is at distance r from three adjacent flat faces. At the junction, both surfaces pass through the same vertices with the same curvature.

So as BFS grows from a sphere seed at a corner:
1. **Vertex distance check**: cylinder fillet vertices are within  of the sphere because they genuinely lie on (or very close to) a sphere of the same radius centered at the corner. The further you go from the corner along the fillet, the further vertices deviate — but on a coarse mesh, the next row of fillet vertices may still be within tolerance.
2. **Centroid check**: face centroids on the cylinder near the corner are also close to the sphere surface, because the sagitta between cylinder and sphere surfaces is small near the tangency line.
3. **Re-fit**: when the sphere re-fits to include cylinder fillet vertices, the fit *improves* or stays similar because those vertices approximately satisfy the sphere equation. The least-squares sphere fit accommodates them by shifting the center slightly, and the new fit still passes all vertices within tolerance.

The re-fit validation **would** catch this if the cylinder fillet curved away from the sphere quickly — but it doesn't, because both surfaces have the same radius and are tangent. The deviation grows as ~ $d^2/(2r)$ where $d$ is the distance along the fillet from the sphere-cylinder boundary, which is tiny for nearby faces.

On a **finer** mesh, more fillet faces are closer to the boundary (smaller face size), so more of them pass the tolerance check. That's why the fine mesh has spheres consuming ~1300 faces each — nearly all the fillet faces are close enough to a corner sphere to pass.

The solid-angle coverage validation at the end is supposed to catch this (detecting that the hypothesis spans a strip rather than a patch), but the fillet faces wrap around 3 cylinder surfaces emanating from each corner, giving the direction vectors enough 3D spread to pass the eigenvalue ratio test.
